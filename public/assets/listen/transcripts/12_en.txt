The words stop in the throat and do not come out. And when they do, they come as a whisper in the little girl’s ear: ‘The house is yours, Baba. Yours, Mama’s, and Alaa’s. I built it for you. There is a room for you with two beds, one for you and one for Alaa. Didn’t Mama make you a wardrobe too?’

Yes, Ola, my little beloved, it is yours. Everything is yours. Even my being here—I wanted that to be for you. ‘Smile,’ says Randa. The little girl tries, but the smile freezes on her sick little lips and does not move. Why, my child? Why does the smile fall away from your lips? Is it sadness?

Or is it worry?

Or both together?

Where did childhood go? Was it lost between the prison walls, or did the illness take it away?

My fingers tightened. Even the pen shook me. Signing Ola’s school certificate was torture, a heavy burden, and the words disappeared.

O ugly world, O cruel world, O miserable life—how much can a person carry? Hope, God, hope come back. Hardened love, move. Human being, be human.

12-2-91

The image of sick Ola and her gentle voice still control me. No matter how I try to escape, if I succeed in the day, I fail at night. It is one dream over and over: meeting her and Randa in different places. It is one dream spread across many places and times.

Ola’s words were too strong for them to bear without tears. Even beloved Randa—full of life, hopeful, strong, always smiling—lost her smile, and sadness almost took over when Ola’s words and tears struck her.

It was the first time Alaa brought flowers to me while Ola was not there. Where was her mind? What did she want to say? Then after hesitation she said, ‘We do not want to go back home without Baba.’

Do you think, my dear, that I could delay even for a few seconds if I were able to hold you to my chest? No, my beloved.

It is another beautiful image of you, my dear child, that I add to my memory and to my heart. Will your image stay with me until I see you again? I hope what happened was only passing, and that you will return to your old energy and movement. I hope it was only one special moment.

Little Alaa told the elephant-and-ant joke again. He said to Ola for the first time in front of me, ‘Isn’t that right, Ola?’ He was asking her opinion about something. Ola was weighed down by illness and sadness, and part of that sadness had moved into Alaa and showed in his eyes. It seemed there was a secret wire between the two children, carrying part of Ola’s sadness to him. He seemed happy when Ola tried to smile.

Her smile was only half a smile, mixed with tears and a lump in the throat, the same lump that rose in my own throat.

7-3-91

‘You ask me why I love you.’ A little earlier Fairuz was singing, ‘Do not leave me, do not forget me, O sun of the poor.’ But he left us and forgot us. Suddenly the family came for a visit, two days earlier than expected. My beard was long and the gifts were not ready.

The beginning of the visit was not as it should have been, but it did not last long. Soon the atmosphere became normal again. My mother and father came with Randa and the children, and the others as well. There was nothing new; everyone’s health and general condition were fine.

Ola was like a real flower that day, completely different from the previous visit. She was cheerful, lively, and active. She told me she was fine, comfortable at home, and that she liked her room and played in it.

She brought me greetings from her school friends.

She played a game with Alaa, linking fingers. She was happy with the pencil case and the bag I brought her. She said she loved her grandfather and grandmother, Abu Nidal and Umm Nidal, and that they loved her very much.

Little Alaa agreed to play with me for a bit.

He was a very active child, and mischief showed clearly in his eyes. His mother asked me to tell him not to trouble his grandmother. She said he sometimes threatened to pull the house apart or tear his clothes. He moved like a little acrobat and tried hard to break the string.

Beloved Randa was missing nothing. She was active and full of life, though I felt that tiredness and the years had started to show on her dear face.

At the end of the visit, I asked my mother about her relationship with Randa, and she answered that she loved her as she loved me.

13-3-91

Now I am speaking about another matter: the general atmosphere of optimism that controls the wing, most of the comrades here, maybe all of them. On the evening of 11/3, the Palestinian Popular Committees detainees were released, and the next morning all Palestinian political detainees were released, except some groups as I heard.

How beautiful freedom is. How beautiful it is to meet it after long separation. It is the beauty that pushes a person toward optimism. It is the beauty of meeting loved ones—wife, beloved, children, family. The beauty of the home, the town, the work. The beauty of meeting nature, the earth, and the sky.

Many stay up late and cannot sleep. Hand work is almost gone, and interest in it is very weak.

Even food and drink were neglected. Freedom—yes, it is that, or rather the hope of it, the waiting for it. What will it be like if it comes to us? I truly cannot answer.

7-4-91

It was a beautiful day because it held a beautiful visit. Now it is night, and the music program is on the radio.

It was, if I may say so, the complete visit: the timing was good, the overall mood was good, everyone was in good condition, the conversation was good, and the connection with Randa in particular was excellent.
