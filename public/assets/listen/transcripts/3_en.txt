23-12-91

Today a new group of the Brothers was released, and there is still nothing new about us. We received news confirming the release of detainees from Palestine Branch, while some detainees in Adra remained with Political Security. Visits were opened at Military Police, and three visits came to our wing today. The prison director tells us not to be optimistic and not to be pessimistic.

At noon I was trying to convince myself that I had to prepare to spend New Year’s Eve here, and to prepare for more months without freedom. Yes, I started to tear down all the dreams I had built, especially those about the first period of my freedom, like celebrating New Year’s Eve.

Today I also thought a lot about the condition of the loved ones outside, the ‘free’ ones. That thought hurts me deeply and sometimes makes me very tense.

In the evening I read Jabra’s novel Hunters in a Narrow Street. I kept holding it until I finished it. It helped move me into other worlds, but many of its words and images brought me back to where I am, and even further.

The wing will become more tense in the coming days if things stay like this. The cigarettes are running out, the food supplies are very low, and the visits are slow and bring almost nothing. How much longer will our living conditions get worse, and our anxiety and tension rise?

These days are hell itself: constant watching, endless waiting.

This evening I fell again into deep depression, to the point that I moved away from everyone and wanted to speak to no one at all. I did not want to talk, read, or listen to the radio. I did not want anything.

But little by little this state began to fade until it ended. I had not read anything for days, but today I brought Jabra Jabra’s The Hunters and started reading it. I read half of it this evening. That is not my usual way of reading literature. My overall state is better now.

My wish is still the same: that these be my last pages written here. Will this day, of which more than half an hour has passed, make that wish come true?

22-12-91

I opened my bag, took out my notebook, glued the papers from the past days onto it, and decided to continue writing in it. We have become like a sheet in the wind, or a piece of cork thrown about by rough sea waves. One piece of news takes us one way, another brings us back. Where are the mental constants we are supposed to stand on? In moments of emotional shock everything disappears. We forget everything. Sometimes I laugh when my nerves cool down and I become calm again, and I think about what happened and how I dealt with what I heard.

I feel happy just discussing the news and trying to make sure it is true and accurate. Of course, trying does not always mean succeeding.

Things happened today much like the previous days. A visit came for Nidal, and the main question was whether visits had really been opened again. The answer was a bitter disappointment for the wing, even both wings: it was an ordinary visit, and some saw that as a negative sign about release from prison.

A new group of about 200 of the Brothers was released today, but none of us were included. The continued release of people is positive, but the negative side—how far it still is from us—was what dominated the mood.

The news from the visit saying that not all Political Security detainees were released was also upsetting.

The news about the families outside upset me too. My family, like Nidal’s family and the families of the other young men, seem to be living through a deadly state of anxiety and waiting.

24-12-91

It is Christmas Eve, for the fifth year in a row. I was lucky today: I drank a glass of good locally made wine. Even though it was small and there was very little of it, it felt special.

Ten days have now passed since the start of the storm. That may sound short, but because of what happened in those days, it is a long time. For our feelings, it is a very, very long time.

It is Christmas Eve. How wonderful it could have been if it had come with freedom, if we were outside this place now, if we could drink a real glass of wine—or several. I feel a deep sadness controlling every cell of my body. My feelings are very contradictory.

I remember Randa, dear Randa. I remember her words when she said, ‘I am afraid.’ Yes, I am afraid that I will spend New Year’s Eve again far away from you, my beloved woman.

If tomorrow passes—the holiday—as this day passed, then things will not be well. I think despair is sweeping over most of us. Many will think that everything has stopped now, that they have already released everyone they wanted to release for the time being. I think that is what many will believe.

This evening I heard Fairuz singing ‘Am yil‘abu al-wlad.’ I love this song very much. I enjoyed it deeply. I lived through dreamy minutes with Ola, Alaa, and Randa. We ran under the blue sky, and I covered the two little ones while they slept so they would not feel cold.

I had dreamed of decorating a beautiful Christmas tree for the two little ones at home. I had planned it before and imagined how happy dear Ola and Alaa would be.

Now we are in the first hour of the holiday. May all the loved ones be safe and well—first of all wonderful Randa, dear Ola and Alaa, my kind parents, all my brothers and sisters, and all the people I love.

And now—how much longer will this torture continue? How much longer will this terrible madness continue, and why? Does the person doing this understand how much pain, sadness, and humiliation each passing day causes us while we are in this condition?

Life is harsh, harsher than I imagine. Living seems to require struggle, endurance, and a real will to live. But until when? How much can a person bear, and how far can he resist?

And in the end—what? I go back to being a feather in the wind. I do not know how long I will remain this way, or those with me. I feel hatred, pain, anger, and despair, and also strength, forgiveness, love, and optimism—all at the same time. Is that not frightening?
